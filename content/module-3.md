# Module 3: Grey Zones

This is the full text for Module 3, written as ambassadors will see it. It covers the main forms of misconduct and the cases where the line isn't obvious.

## Unit 3.1 Is This Plagiarism?

*About 10 minutes. You'll learn what makes a paraphrase acceptable, and why citing a source isn't always enough.*

### What plagiarism is

Plagiarism means presenting someone else's work or ideas as your own without proper acknowledgement. It isn't only copying without a reference. A good paraphrase needs four steps: read the source, understand it, rewrite it in your own words and structure, and cite it. Changing a few words in someone else's sentence, even with a citation, is often called patchwriting. It's a very common stage in learning to write academically, but many institutions still treat it as poor practice or, if it's extensive, as plagiarism.

### Case file

Nina is writing a literature review. For each source, she takes the key sentences, swaps some words for synonyms, changes the order slightly and adds the citation. "I always cite," she says. "So it can't be plagiarism."

### Decision point

How would you judge Nina's approach?

### Debrief

- **A. It's fine, she cites everything.** Citing is essential, and Nina clearly isn't trying to hide her sources. But the citation tells the reader whose idea it is, not whose words. If the sentences are still mostly the author's, they should be quoted or properly rewritten.
- **B. It's plagiarism.** Some teachers would see it that way, especially if most of the review is built like this. But calling it plagiarism straight away ignores that Nina is trying to do the right thing and hasn't learned how yet.
- **C. It's poor academic writing, not misconduct.** Often the fairest reading, as long as it's not extensive. It still needs fixing, because the reader can't see Nina's own understanding, and that's what the assignment is for.
- **D. It depends on the course rules.** Expectations do differ, and some fields use quotes more than others. But no course rewards text that is mostly someone else's with a few words changed.

As an ambassador, the most useful thing you can do for a student like Nina is to explain the difference and point her to a writing centre or study skills support, not to label her.

### Your turn: spot the difference

Here's an original sentence from a (fictional) source by Novak (2024):

> "Students who plan their writing early are less likely to feel pressure near deadlines, and pressure is one of the most common reasons for cutting corners."

Which of these versions is acceptable?

1. Students who plan their writing early are less likely to feel pressure near deadlines (Novak, 2024).
2. Students who organise their writing early are less likely to experience stress close to deadlines (Novak, 2024).
3. According to Novak (2024), planning ahead reduces the deadline pressure that often pushes students to cut corners.

**Answer:** Only version 3. Version 1 copies the exact words, so it needs quotation marks. Version 2 is patchwriting: the structure and most of the words are still Novak's. Version 3 says the same idea in new words and structure, with the source cited.

### Go deeper (for postgraduates and PhDs)

In research, misconduct goes beyond plagiarism. Fabrication means inventing data or results, and falsification means manipulating them, for example by leaving out inconvenient data. Other questionable practices include splitting one study into several minimal papers (sometimes called salami publishing) and publishing the same results twice without saying so.

### Takeaway

A citation shows whose idea it is. Your own words show that you understood it.

## Unit 3.2 Collaboration or Collusion?

*About 9 minutes. You'll learn where working together turns into collusion, and how to help peers stay on the right side of the line.*

### The difference

Learning together is one of the best ways to learn, and most teachers encourage it. **Collaboration** is working together on something that is meant to be shared, or helping each other understand. **Collusion** is working together on something that is meant to be individual, so that the submitted work no longer shows one person's own effort. The line depends on the task, not on how friendly the group is.

### Case file

Luca, Zofia and Ahmed study together for weeks. They discuss the readings, plan their essays side by side, and sometimes share paragraphs "just to compare". Each submits an individual essay. The teacher notices the three essays have the same structure, the same examples and some identical sentences.

### Decision point

Where was the line?

### Debrief

- **A. Studying together is always fine.** Studying together is fine, and they shouldn't stop. But sharing paragraphs and planning the same structure goes beyond studying. It shapes the essays themselves.
- **B. Similar structure means collusion.** Similarity alone doesn't prove collusion: students who attend the same lectures often make similar points. Here, though, the identical sentences suggest the essays were partly written together.
- **C. It depends on whether the work was meant to be individual.** This is the key question. For an individual essay, discussing ideas is collaboration, but sharing written text and building essays together is collusion.
- **D. They should have asked the teacher first.** The best way to avoid the problem. Teachers can say exactly what's allowed, and most are glad to be asked.

### Your turn: OK, ask first, or not OK?

For an individual assignment, sort each of these:

1. Discussing the assignment topic over coffee
2. Explaining a difficult concept to a classmate
3. Showing a classmate your essay outline
4. Writing answers together and each submitting a copy
5. Checking each other's final answers before submitting

**How others see it:** most teachers would say 1 and 2 are OK, 3 and 5 are "ask first", and 4 is not OK. Rules differ, which is why "ask first" is such a useful category.

### Takeaway

Share ideas, not answers. When the work is individual and you're unsure, ask first.

## Unit 3.3 Reusing Your Own Work

*About 7 minutes. You'll learn why reusing your own earlier work can be a problem, and how to do it the right way.*

### Why your own work can count

It can feel strange that you might need permission to use your own work. The reason is that each assignment is meant to show new learning for that course. Submitting the same work twice, without saying so, presents old work as new. This is often called self-plagiarism or duplicate submission. Building on your earlier work is usually fine, as long as you're open about it and the rules allow it.

### Case file

Last year, Petra wrote an essay on social media and democracy that got an excellent grade. This year, a different course asks for an essay on a similar topic. She's busy with exams and considers submitting the same essay with a new title page.

### Decision point

Is it acceptable?

### Debrief

- **A. Yes, it's her own work.** No one else's ideas are involved, which is why many students don't see a problem. But the new course expects new work, and the essay was already graded once.
- **B. No, it's self-plagiarism.** Submitting it unchanged and without saying so is generally treated as self-plagiarism, even though the work is hers.
- **C. Only with permission.** The safest route. Some teachers will allow reuse, or suggest she expands the earlier essay. Asking takes one email.
- **D. Only if she rewrites it.** Rewriting helps, but if the ideas and structure are the same, it's still reuse. Building on old work is fine when she's open about it, for example by citing her earlier essay.

### Your turn: add to your answer kit

Find what your institution says about reusing your own work. Look for words like "self-plagiarism", "duplicate submission" or "reuse" in the integrity policy you saved in Module 1. Add the link or a one-sentence summary to your answer kit. If you can't find anything, note that too, because that's useful information for ENAI.

### Go deeper (for postgraduates and PhDs)

In research, reusing your own published text, data or figures without citing them is a recognised integrity issue. Publishing the same results twice, or reusing large parts of earlier papers, can mislead readers and editors. Building a thesis on your own earlier papers is common, but each reuse should be cited and follow the rules of your institution and the publishers involved.

### Takeaway

You can build on your own work. Just be open about it, and ask when you're not sure.

## Unit 3.4 Sharing Your Notes and Old Assignments

*About 8 minutes. You'll learn when sharing helps others learn and when it can get both of you into trouble.*

### Helping or facilitating?

Sharing lecture notes, explaining things and recommending sources are good ways to help other students. Sharing graded work is different. If someone copies from it, that's misconduct, and helping someone commit misconduct is called facilitation. In many institutions, the person who shared the work can face consequences too, even if they meant well.

### Case file

Ali, a first-year student, messages you:

> "Hey! You took this course last year, right? Could you send me your graded essay? Just for inspiration, I won't copy, promise 🙏"

You got a good grade and you'd like to help.

### Decision point

What do you do?

### Debrief

- **A. Send it.** It's generous, and Ali may honestly mean "just inspiration". But once you send it, you lose control over how it's used. If parts end up in his essay, you could both be involved in a misconduct case.
- **B. Refuse.** It protects you, but a plain "no" can feel unfriendly, and Ali still needs help.
- **C. Share tips instead of the essay.** A thoughtful middle way. Tell him what the teacher valued, like a clear structure or good sources, and what you'd do differently, so he can learn from your mistakes. He gets real guidance, but no text to copy.
- **D. Point him to official support.** Suggest he looks at the assessment criteria, asks the teacher whether example essays are available, or visits the writing centre. It's reliable help that can't get either of you into trouble. It works best combined with C, so he doesn't feel brushed off.

### Your turn

Write your own list of places where students like Ali can get help with academic writing and referencing at your institution. Look for:

1. A writing centre, academic skills service or library support for writing
2. Guides or workshops on referencing and citation styles
3. Any online courses or self-study materials on academic writing your institution offers

Next time someone asks for your essay, you'll have something better to offer. If your institution has none of these, note that too, because it's useful information for ENAI.

### Takeaway

Share what helps someone learn, not work they could copy.

## Unit 3.5 The 23% Report

*About 9 minutes. You'll learn how to read a similarity report, so you can calm down a panicking friend with facts.*

### What similarity tools really do

Text-matching tools, often wrongly called "plagiarism detectors", compare a text with other sources and highlight passages that match. They don't decide whether something is plagiarism. A match can be a correctly quoted and cited sentence, a reference list, a common phrase, or the assignment's own template. A person, usually the teacher, looks at what is matched and decides. The percentage on its own says very little.

### Case file

Your friend Tereza calls you, close to tears:

> "I uploaded my thesis draft and the similarity report says 23%! Am I going to be accused of plagiarism? Should I rewrite everything tonight?"

### Decision point

What do you tell her?

### Debrief

- **A. 23% means plagiarism.** This would only add to her panic, and it's not how the reports work. There's no universal "safe" percentage.
- **B. Look at what is matched, not the number.** The most useful advice. Help her open the report and check the highlighted passages: are they quotes with citations, references, standard phrases, or real unacknowledged text?
- **C. Rewrite until it's under 10%.** Chasing a number often leads to awkward rewording of perfectly fine text, or even to patchwriting. It fixes the score, not the writing.
- **D. Ask the teacher how they read reports.** Good advice alongside B. Many supervisors have their own way of reading reports, and asking shows good faith.

### Your turn: which match is a problem?

Tereza's report highlights these five passages. Which ones need fixing?

1. Her reference list at the end
2. A sentence in quotation marks, with the citation
3. The phrase "the results of this study suggest that"
4. A full paragraph matching a website, with no citation
5. A paragraph from her own earlier draft, uploaded to the same system last month

**Answer:** Only 4 clearly needs fixing. 1, 2 and 3 are normal matches. 5 is usually fine, but she should mention it to her supervisor so they aren't confused by it.

### Takeaway

The report shows similarity, not plagiarism. Read what's matched before you worry about the number.

## Unit 3.6 A Message During the Exam

*About 7 minutes. You'll prepare for a moment many students face, and practise saying no without losing a friend.*

### Exams and facilitation

Using unauthorised help during an exam is cheating. Giving that help is facilitation, and in most institutions both students can face consequences. Online exams can feel less "real" than sitting in an exam hall, and messages arrive in the same apps you use every day. That's exactly why it helps to decide in advance what you'll do.

### Case file

You're taking an online exam at home. Halfway through, your phone lights up. It's your classmate Mateo:

> "What did you put for question 4?? I'm totally stuck, just the answer pls 😭"

### Decision point

What do you do?

### Debrief

- **A. Send it, it's just one answer.** It feels small and kind. But it's facilitation, and it puts both of you at risk. It also isn't fair to the rest of the class.
- **B. Ignore the message.** You stay out of it, and many people choose this in the moment. Mateo may feel ignored, so a word after the exam can help.
- **C. Reply that you can't.** Clear and honest. A short "Sorry, can't during the exam" protects you both and sets a boundary without drama.
- **D. Talk to him after the exam.** A kind follow-up, whatever you did during it. You could offer to study together for the next one, which addresses why he was stuck in the first place.

Many ambassadors combine C and D: a short no now, and support later.

### Your turn: your boundary sentence

Write one sentence you could send when someone asks for answers during an exam. Make it sound like you, friendly but clear. For example:

> "Sorry, I don't share answers during exams. Let's study together before the next one?"

### Takeaway

Decide before the exam what you'll say. A friendly no now, and help later.
