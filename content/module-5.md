# Module 5: Contract Cheating and Essay Mills

This is the full text for Module 5, written as ambassadors will see it. It covers how essay mills reach students, the risks for students who use them, and how ambassadors can respond.

## Unit 5.1 The Friendly Tutor

*About 8 minutes. You'll learn what contract cheating is and how to tell real tutoring apart from an essay mill.*

### What contract cheating is

Contract cheating means getting someone else to do your academic work for you, whether you pay them or not. The companies that sell this are often called essay mills. They rarely call themselves that. Instead, they offer "academic help", "proofreading", "tutoring" or "model answers", and they reach students where they already are: social media, messaging apps and search results, especially close to deadlines. Real tutoring helps you learn to do the work yourself. An essay mill does the work for you.

### Case file

Two days before an essay deadline, you get a direct message on Instagram from an account called @StudyHelpPro:

> "Hi! 👋 Stressed about deadlines? We offer academic help & proofreading by expert writers. 100% original, delivered in 24h. First order 20% off! ✨"

The account has thousands of followers and lots of positive reviews.

### Decision point

What do you think is really being offered?

### Debrief

- **A. Legitimate tutoring.** The friendly tone and reviews make it look that way. But tutors don't promise "100% original" texts delivered in 24 hours. That describes a finished product, not help with learning.
- **B. Contract cheating.** Most likely. "Expert writers", "original" and a 24-hour delivery point to writing the work for you, whatever the advert calls it.
- **C. Hard to tell without more information.** A fair instinct, and some services do sit in a grey zone. But a quick look usually settles it: real tutors talk about explaining and practising, not about delivering texts.
- **D. A scam.** It could be both. Some services take the money and deliver poor or copied work, or nothing at all. Others keep students' details and use them later, which Unit 5.3 covers.

### Your turn: spot the red flags

Which of these phrases are warning signs of an essay mill?

1. "100% plagiarism-free, written from scratch"
2. "We explain difficult concepts in live sessions"
3. "Guaranteed grade or your money back"
4. "Send us your assignment brief and deadline"
5. "Model answers for reference only"
6. "We help you plan and improve your own draft"

**Answer:** 1, 3, 4 and 5 are red flags. They describe finished work produced for you, and "for reference only" is a common disclaimer that rarely changes how the work is used. 2 and 6 describe real learning support, though it's still worth checking who offers it and what your institution allows.

### Takeaway

Real help teaches you to do the work. An essay mill does it for you, whatever it calls itself.

## Unit 5.2 Inside the WhatsApp Group

*About 8 minutes. You'll practise responding when essay mills advertise in student spaces, and you'll start your field mission.*

### How essay mills reach students

Essay mills often join student group chats, comment under university social media posts, or pose as fellow students. They know when deadlines and exam periods are, and they time their messages to match. Ambassadors are often among the first to notice, which makes you useful as an early warning, without needing to confront anyone.

### Case file

Your course has a WhatsApp group with over 200 students. One evening, a new member you don't recognise posts:

> "Struggling with your assignments? 📚 Our team of graduates can help with essays, reports and presentations. DM for a quote, use code EXAM10 for 10% off!"

A few people react with a thumbs up.

### Decision point

What do you do?

### Debrief

- **A. Ignore it.** It's not your group to manage, and many people will scroll past. But some classmates may take the offer seriously, especially if no one says anything.
- **B. Warn the group.** A short, friendly message can help, for example that services like this can lead to misconduct cases and scams. Keep it calm and factual, without lecturing anyone.
- **C. Report it to the group admin or the course.** Often the most effective step. The admin can remove the post and the account, and the course team may want to know that essay mills target their students.
- **D. Leave the group.** It protects you from the noise, but you lose a useful channel, and the advert stays.

Many ambassadors combine B and C: a calm note to the group and a quiet message to the admin.

### Your turn: field mission

Over the next few weeks, keep an eye out for one essay-mill advert aimed at students at your institution. It could be on social media, in a group chat, on a poster or in search results. When you find one, describe it here in a few sentences: where you saw it, what it promised, and how it tried to look trustworthy. You can also upload a screenshot, but remove any names or personal details first.

Your examples help ENAI see how essay mills target students around the world.

### Takeaway

You don't need to confront anyone. Notice, report, and help others see what the advert really is.

## Unit 5.3 After the Purchase

*About 8 minutes. You'll learn how to support a student who is being pressured by an essay mill, calmly and without judgement.*

### A risk students rarely hear about

Some essay mills contact students long after a purchase and demand more money, threatening to tell the university if they don't pay. This is blackmail, and it's a crime, even if the student did something wrong first. Paying rarely ends it, and often leads to more demands. Students in this situation usually feel ashamed and alone, which is exactly what the blackmailer counts on. A calm, non-judgemental listener can make a real difference.

### Case file

A student you don't know well sends you a message, because they know you're an ambassador:

> "I need advice but please don't judge me. Last year I paid a website for an essay. Now they're messaging me saying they'll send everything to my university unless I pay €300. I don't know what to do."

### Decision point

What would you suggest first?

### Debrief

- **A. Pay, to make it go away.** Understandable when someone is scared. But paying usually shows the blackmailer that the threat works, and the demands tend to continue.
- **B. Ignore the messages.** Not engaging is part of good advice: they shouldn't reply or negotiate. But ignoring it alone leaves them carrying the stress without support.
- **C. Seek confidential help.** The most helpful first step. Student counselling, a student advice service or the student union can help them think through options in confidence, including whether and how to talk to the university.
- **D. Report it to the police.** Blackmail is a crime, and reporting is a legitimate option, especially if threats continue. It can feel like a big step, so confidential support (C) can help them decide.

### How you can help

Listen without judging. Tell them they're not alone and that the pressure they're under is a crime. Suggest that they keep screenshots of all the messages, stop replying, and don't pay. Then help them find confidential support. You don't have to handle it alone either: you can contact ENAI for backup at any time.

### Your turn: true or false?

1. Paying the blackmailer will make it stop.
2. Blackmail is a crime, even if the student bought an essay.
3. It's a good idea to keep screenshots of the messages.
4. The student has to deal with this alone.

**Answer:** 1 is false, 2 is true, 3 is true and 4 is false.

### Takeaway

Don't pay, don't reply, keep the evidence, and get confidential help. Nobody has to face this alone.

## Unit 5.4 Why It Spreads

*About 8 minutes. You'll explore why "everyone does it" is such a powerful belief, and how ambassadors can gently challenge it.*

### The power of "everyone does it"

Research on academic integrity has found again and again that students who believe cheating is common are more likely to cheat themselves. Often the belief is bigger than the reality: a few loud stories make it seem normal. That's why one of the most effective things ambassadors can do is change what students think is normal, by showing that most students do their own work and that support is available.

### Case file

At lunch, a classmate says:

> "Honestly, everyone buys essays these days. The university can't check them all, nobody ever gets caught. You'd be stupid to struggle when you can just pay."

A few people at the table laugh. Some look unsure.

### Decision point

How do you respond?

### Debrief

- **A. Agree that it's common.** Going along with it is easy, but it reinforces the belief that cheating is normal, which is exactly what helps it spread.
- **B. Share what you know about the risks.** Facts can help, for example that essay mills can blackmail students or deliver poor work. Keep it light, or it can sound like a lecture.
- **C. Point out what they'd lose.** Reminding them that a degree is meant to show real skills, like in Unit 2.1, can work well, especially with students who care about their future careers.
- **D. Ask what pressure they're under.** Often the most powerful response. "Everyone does it" can hide stress or fear of failing. A curious question opens a real conversation and leads into Module 6.

### Your turn: a culture of honest work

Think about how you, as an ambassador, could help to challenge the idea that "everyone does it". Answer three short questions:

1. Is it really true that "everyone does it"? Why do some students believe it?
2. Think of the students you know. Do most of them do their own work?
3. What is one simple thing you could do to show that most students do their own work?

There are no right answers. Good ideas may grow into activities.

### Takeaway

Most students do their own work. Help others see that, and offer help, not judgement.
