# Module 6: Why Students Cut Corners

This is the full text for Module 6, written as ambassadors will see it. It covers the pressures behind misconduct and how to respond to peers with empathy, not judgement.

## Unit 6.1 Three Deadlines, One Night

*About 7 minutes. You'll see how pressure leads to shortcuts, and what helps a student who is running out of time.*

### Pressure, not bad intentions

Most students who cut corners aren't planning to cheat. They run out of time. Part-time jobs, several deadlines in the same week, family duties or simply poor planning can leave a student at midnight with work they can't finish. In that moment, a shortcut can feel like the only way out. Knowing the other options is what helps.

### Case file

Martina works 20 hours a week in a café to pay her rent. This week, she has three deadlines on the same day. It's Wednesday night, and one lab report isn't started. An older student offers to send her his report from last year:

> "Just change the numbers and the wording a bit, nobody will notice."

### Decision point

What would help Martina most?

### Debrief

- **A. Ask for an extension.** Often possible, especially with a reason like work commitments, and many students never ask. The earlier she asks, the better her chances.
- **B. Get study skills support.** Helpful in the long run, for planning and time management. It won't solve tonight, though, so it works best together with A or C.
- **C. Prioritise and accept a lower grade on one.** A realistic option. A weaker report she wrote herself is far better than a good one that isn't hers, which could lead to a misconduct case.
- **D. Talk to the teacher.** Similar to A, and teachers are often more understanding than students expect. Even a short, honest email can open options.

Whatever she chooses, copying the old report would turn a time problem into an integrity problem.

### Your turn

What would you say to Martina first? Write it in one or two sentences.

### Takeaway

Running out of time is a problem with solutions. A shortcut only creates a bigger one.

## Unit 6.2 Fear of Failing

*About 8 minutes. You'll practise supporting a stressed peer while staying within your role, and find the support available at your institution.*

### Wellbeing and integrity

Stress, anxiety and fear of failure are closely linked to cutting corners. A student who feels they can't afford to fail is more likely to see cheating as a way out. As an ambassador, you can listen and help them find support, but you're not a counsellor. Knowing where to send someone is one of the most valuable things you can do. For more on this, see the ENAI Wellbeing Series below.

Explore the [ENAI Wellbeing Series](https://www.academicintegrity.eu/wp/wellbeing-series/) for more on how wellbeing and academic integrity are connected.

### Case file

Your classmate Jakub failed an important exam last semester. If he fails the retake, he loses his scholarship. Over coffee, he says:

> "I can't fail again. My family is counting on this. Honestly, I'll do whatever it takes to pass."

He looks exhausted.

### Decision point

How do you respond?

### Debrief

- **A. Reassure him.** ("You'll be fine!") Kindly meant, but it can feel like you're not taking him seriously. He needs to feel heard before he can feel reassured.
- **B. Share your own experience.** It can help him feel less alone, if you've been through something similar. Keep it short, so the conversation stays about him.
- **C. Point him to counselling or student support.** Important, especially as he seems exhausted and under heavy pressure. You could offer to help him find the right contact. If someone seems to be in serious distress, encourage them to get professional help straight away.
- **D. Help him make a plan.** Practical and calming: when to study, who to ask for help, and whether a study advisor could help him prepare. It also gently answers "whatever it takes" with a better way.

The best response usually starts with listening, then combines C and D.

### Your turn: field mission

Find out where students at your institution can get help with stress or mental health. Look for:

1. Counselling or psychological support
2. Who students can contact outside office hours or in an emergency
3. Any peer support or mentoring programmes

Add them to your answer kit from Unit 1.4.

### Takeaway

Listen first. Then help them find support, because you don't have to carry it for them.

## Unit 6.3 Different Rules Back Home

*About 7 minutes. You'll see how unclear expectations can lead to misconduct, and how you can help a student understand the rules.*

### When the rules aren't clear

Academic conventions aren't the same everywhere. In some education systems, repeating an expert's words without a reference is a sign of respect, or citing is only expected in research papers. Students who move between countries, or from school to university, can break rules they never knew existed. They still need to follow the rules where they study now, but they deserve a clear explanation, not just a penalty.

### Case file

Wei has just started a master's programme abroad. In his first essay, he includes several paragraphs from textbooks without citing them. At his previous university, this was normal for coursework, and he thought he was showing he'd read the key texts. His teacher flags the essay for plagiarism.

### Decision point

Is this misconduct?

### Debrief

- **A. Yes, rules are rules.** By the rules of his new institution, it is plagiarism. But treating it exactly like deliberate cheating ignores that Wei didn't know.
- **B. No, it's a learning gap.** Wei clearly didn't mean to deceive anyone. Many institutions treat first cases like this as a chance to learn, but the essay still breaks the rules.
- **C. It depends on intent.** Intent often affects how a case is handled, for example a warning instead of a penalty. It doesn't change what the essay is.
- **D. It shows the institution should explain better.** A fair point. If new students aren't taught the expectations, cases like Wei's will keep happening. Ambassadors can help close this gap.

### Your turn

In two or three simple sentences, think about international students on your campus and answer:

1. What challenges do you think they may face with academic rules and expectations?
2. What could help them, from you as an ambassador or from your institution?

### Takeaway

Not everyone starts with the same rules. A clear explanation helps more than a quick judgement.
