## Unit 1.1 Welcome to ENAI

*About 6 minutes. You'll learn what ENAI is, what your year as an ambassador looks like, and what support you can count on.*

### Case file

Thank you for choosing to become an ENAI Student Ambassador. It's an important commitment: you're stepping up to support other students and taking responsibility for keeping the values of academic integrity alive at your institution. Nobody expects you to be perfect. Ambassadors make mistakes and learn from them too, and that's part of what makes you approachable. Before you start, here's what you've joined.

**ENAI** is the European Network for Academic Integrity. It's a non-profit organisation, legally based in Czechia, with members from around the world. Its members are institutions and individuals who support academic integrity. ENAI runs working groups, publishes resources, and organizes ENAI summer school and the European Conference on Ethics and Integrity in Academia (ECEIA) every year.

Visit [academicintegrity.eu](https://www.academicintegrity.eu) to see who our members are (your institution might be one of them), which projects we're working on, and what else ENAI does.

**Your role** has four sides:

- **First point of contact.** Students can come to you with questions about academic integrity.
- **Promoter.** You raise awareness through events, campaigns and creative ideas.
- **Active learner.** You join ENAI training and meetings and keep growing your own knowledge.
- **Connector.** You represent ENAI at your institution and bring local perspectives back to the international team.

**T****h****e A****mbass****ador ****Year** runs from fall 2026 to fall 2027, but the ambassadors can join at any point during it. Over the year, you organize at least one activity and answer peer questions that may come to your email. Online meetings are optional, but they're a good way to learn and to meet other ambassadors. To receive the certificate at the end of the year, you need to complete this training and show your activity in your activity log.

**What you get from ENAI:** support for your activities from the international network, backup when you're unsure how to answer something, templates and materials, extra training and consultations with international experts, a safe space to share your wins and your struggles, the chance to attend ENAI events and be invited to the ENAI Summer School or the ECEIA conference, a badge when you finish this training, and a certificate at the end of the year.

### Decision point

Which part of the role excites you most?

### Debrief

- **A. Running events.** You'll probably shine as a promoter. Workshops, panels and poster days reach many students at once. Your one required activity is a good place to start, so think about which event your institution is missing.
- **B. Supporting peers.** You're drawn to being a first point of contact. That's often the most valuable part of the role, because a student who is confused or worried is more likely to talk to a peer than a teacher. Units 1.2 to 1.4 prepare you for exactly this.
- **C. Online campaigns.** Social media reaches students where they already are, especially before deadlines and exam periods. Short, positive messages tend to work better than warnings.
- **D. Research and policy.** You're a natural connector. Ambassadors can bring student views into ENAI working groups and help map how policies work at their institutions, which you'll start doing in Module 7.

There's no wrong choice here. Most ambassadors end up doing a bit of everything, but knowing where your energy is helps you plan your year.

### Takeaway

You're joining a network, not working alone. Use the support it offers.

## Unit 1.2 Ambassador, Not Police

*About 4 minutes. You'll practise explaining your role in a way that makes people want to come to you.*

### What academic integrity is about

Academic integrity means learning, teaching and doing research according to six values: honesty, trust, fairness, respect, responsibility and courage. In practice, it means doing your own work, acknowledging other people's ideas, and making choices based on these values rather than on convenience or pressure. It matters because it makes a degree mean something: it shows real learning, and it lets everyone trust research results. The same values don't stop at the university gate. They shape how we work, how we treat others, and whether people can rely on us in everyday life.&#32;

What you do, and why

**What you do:** help peers understand the rules, point them to the right support, and promote integrity as something positive.

**What you don't do:** report, investigate or judge anyone. That's the job of teachers and institutions.

**Why it matters:** students usually ask a peer before they ask a teacher. Many cases of misconduct start with confusion about the rules or pressure from deadlines, not bad intentions. A peer they trust can step in early, before a small question becomes a big problem. That only works if students see you as safe to talk to.

### Case file

You mention in your seminar group chat that you've become an ENAI Student Ambassador. A minute later, Lukas replies:

> "Nice. So now you'll report us all? 👀"

Two people react with a laughing emoji. Everyone is waiting to see what you say.

### Decision point

How do you respond?

### Debrief

- **A. Laugh it off.** ("Haha, don't worry 😅") This keeps the mood light and protects your relationships, which matters. But the joke stands unanswered, and some people may quietly believe it. You've missed an easy chance to show what you're really there for.
- **B. Explain the role seriously.** ("Actually, ambassadors don't report anyone. The role is about promoting integrity and…") Everything you say is true, but a formal explanation in a group chat can sound defensive. People may remember the tone more than the content.
- **C. Turn it into a conversation.** ("No 😄 I'm more the person you can ask when the rules are confusing. Like, does anyone actually know what we're allowed to use AI for in this course?") This answers the joke lightly and shows your role in action. It also opens a real question most people have. Keep it casual: if it feels like a lecture, the effect is lost.
- **D. Say nothing.** Avoiding an awkward moment is understandable. But silence lets the "integrity police" image settle, and first impressions decide whether people come to you later with real questions.

Ambassadors are not enforcers. You don't investigate, report or judge. Your value comes from being someone students trust, and that trust starts with how you handle small moments like this one.

### Your turn

Write the reply you would actually send to Lukas, in your own words and style. One to three sentences is enough. Then compare it with the example below.

> Example: "No 😄 Ambassadors don't report anyone. I'm more the person you can ask when the rules are confusing, like what we're actually allowed to use AI for in this course. Does anyone know?"

Your reply is saved with your progress. It isn't graded, and there's no single right version.

### Takeaway

Be the person people can ask, not the person they fear. How you answer the first joke sets the tone.

## Unit 1.3 Where the Role Ends

*About 7 minutes. You'll learn how to support a peer without taking on a role that isn't yours, and what confidentiality means for you.*

### How to support a peer

When someone comes to you, start without judgement. Listen and try to understand what's going on before you say anything. Give only the advice you're sure about, and for everything else, point them to better sources and tell them who to approach.

You don't have to sort out every issue, and you don't need to know everything. **We are here for you.** For extra support, you can always:

- message Veronika on WhatsApp: +420 775 586 456
- email ENAI: <info@academicintegrity.eu>

### Case file

You're having coffee with your friend Mia. She goes quiet, then says:

> "Can I tell you something? Last term I paid someone to write one of my essays. Nobody noticed, and I passed. But I can't stop thinking about it. You know about this stuff now. What should I do?"

### Decision point

What's your next step?

### Debrief

- **A. Advise her yourself.** You care, and you want to help right now. But you probably don't know exactly how her institution handles cases like this, and consequences differ a lot. Well-meant advice that turns out to be wrong could make things harder for her. Listening is your job; deciding what she should do is not.
- **B. Point her to student support or an academic advisor.** This keeps you in your role: you listen without judging, then help her find people who can give confidential, informed advice, such as a study advisor, student counselling or the student union. You can offer to help her find the right contact, or to go with her. This is usually the most helpful thing you can do.
- **C. Tell her to confess to the teacher.** Honesty matters, and coming forward can be the right choice. But it's her decision, with real consequences, and she should make it with proper advice first. Pushing her can feel like judgement and may stop her from talking to anyone.
- **D. Keep out of it.** It's fair to feel this is too big for you. But Mia chose to trust you at a vulnerable moment. You don't have to solve it; you can say: "I'm not the right person to advise you, but I'll help you find who is."

### Confidentiality

What a peer tells you in confidence stays with you. Don't share their name, or anything else that could identify them, with friends, teachers, or ENAI. If you need advice from ENAI, describe the situation without details that identify anyone. Anonymized cases are welcome, though. A story that's close to reality, with every identifying detail removed, can help other ambassadors see how someone handled a similar situation.

**How to share a case anonymously.** Even without a name, people can be recognized from details like their course, an assignment or the timing. Before you share a story:

- Remove names, the institution, the course and dates.
- Change details that don't matter to the story, such as gender, subject or country, or blend two cases into one.
- Ask the person's permission if you can.

It's not your job to report misconduct you hear about. Your role is to listen and to point people to the right support.

There are limits. If someone tells you they are being threatened or blackmailed, or you believe someone is at risk of harm, encourage them to get help straight away. If someone's safety is in immediate danger, contact emergency services in your country.&#32;

### Takeaway

Listen, don't judge, and refer. You don't have to fix it alone.

## Unit 1.4 A Question You Can't Answer

*About 12 minutes. You'll learn what to do when a student asks something you don't know, and how to reply so they aren't left alone with it.*

### Questions that aren't yours to answer

Some questions can only be answered by the people responsible: whether a specific tool is allowed in a specific course, how a teacher will assess something, how a misconduct case will be decided, or what a penalty might be. If you answer these and turn out to be wrong, the student may blame you: "But the ambassador said it was fine."

Be careful. Often the best help is to show where the answer can be found, or to look for it together with the student. But the answer itself should come from them and from the people responsible, not from you.

These questions often arrive under pressure, late at night, close to a deadline, from a student who wants the fastest possible answer. It's not your job to be available 24/7. Replying the next day is fine, and so is not having the answer they hoped for. Don't feel bad about it.

### Case file

An email arrives at your ENAI address:

> "Hi, I'm writing my bachelor thesis and I'd like to use Grammarly to check my English and DeepL to translate some sources. My supervisor never said anything about tools. Is that allowed? Thanks, Jana"

You're honestly not sure.

### Decision point

What do you do?

### Debrief

- **A. Give your best guess.** It's quick, and Jana gets an answer. But if your guess is wrong, she may rely on "the ambassador said it was fine" and get into trouble. Rules on tools differ between institutions, and even between courses.
- **B. Point her to her supervisor and course policy.** Her supervisor and her institution's rules are the final authority, so this is the right destination. On its own, though, a redirect can feel like a brush-off. It helps to explain why it depends: correcting grammar is different from translating sources, and much different from generating text.
- **C. Look for the answer together.** ("Let's check your faculty's guidelines together.") This helps Jana without you deciding for her. You find the relevant rules with her, and she takes them to her supervisor for a final answer. Keep the roles clear: you help her search, she gets the answer.
- **D. Reply that it's not your area.** Honest, but it leaves her with nothing. As a first point of contact, you can always at least point someone in the right direction.

The strongest answer usually combines B and C: explain why it depends, help her find where the answer is, and let her supervisor have the final word.

### Your turn: reply to Jana

Write the reply you would send to Jana, in your own words. Three to five sentences is enough. Then compare it with the example below.

### A reply that works

> "Hi Jana, good question, and it's great that you're checking first. The answer depends on your institution and your supervisor, because tools are treated differently. Fixing grammar is usually seen differently from translating sources. I'd suggest emailing your supervisor, saying which tools you'd like to use and for what, and asking for an answer in writing. Whatever you use, mention it in your thesis. If you'd like, I can help you find your institution's guidelines."

### Your turn: build your answer kit

Most questions you'll get can be answered by pointing to the right place. Find these three links for your own institution and save them here:

1. Your institution's academic integrity policy or code
2. Its guidelines on AI and other tools (or note that you couldn't find any)
3. Contacts for student support: a study advisor, counseling or the student union

You'll use this kit all year, and you'll come back to it in Module 7.

### Takeaway

You don't need to know every answer. Know where to find it, and never leave a question unanswered.
