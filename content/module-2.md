# Module 2: Integrity as Values, Not Rules

This is the full text for Module 2, written as ambassadors will see it. Each unit follows the Module 1 pattern: context, case, decision, debrief, your turn, takeaway.

## Unit 2.1 What Is Your Degree Worth?

*About 11 minutes. You'll learn the six values of academic integrity and why breaking them harms more people than just the one who cheats.*

### The six values

Academic integrity rests on six values. You'll meet each of them in this module.

| Value | What it means | In student life |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Honesty | Being truthful in learning, research and communication | Reporting results accurately and citing every source you use |
| Trust | Building confidence between students, teachers and institutions | Being reliable in group work and assessments |
| Fairness | Treating everyone by the same standards | Not gaining an advantage through dishonest means |
| Respect | Acknowledging other people's contributions and ideas | Referencing properly and not presenting others' work as yours |
| Responsibility | Being accountable for your own actions | Meeting deadlines, following the rules, correcting mistakes |
| Courage | Standing by these values under pressure | Speaking up when something isn't right |

### Case file

Elif and Marco graduate from the same programme in the same year, with the same grades. Elif did her own work, including the nights when it was hard. Marco paid someone to write most of his assignments.

A year later, they both apply for the same job. The employer sees two identical diplomas and can't tell them apart.

### Decision point

Who is harmed by what Marco did?

### Debrief

- **A. Only Marco.** Marco is harmed: he has a degree but not the skills behind it, and that usually shows sooner or later. But the harm doesn't stop with him.
- **B. Elif.** Elif's diploma now means less, because it looks exactly like one that was bought. If she and Marco compete for the same job, she isn't competing on fair terms.
- **C. The university and future students.** When cheating becomes known, employers trust graduates of that program less. Every future student carries that reputation, including those who did nothing wrong.
- **D. Nobody, really.** It can feel that way, because nobody seems to notice. But the harm is real even when it's invisible: to Elif, to future students, and potentially to anyone who later relies on Marco's skills, such as a patient, a client or a colleague.

The point isn't to pick one answer. Most people who choose A, B or C are partly right, because integrity is shared: one person's shortcut lowers the value of everyone's degree.

### Case file 2

Daniel studies civil engineering. In a course on structural calculations, he copies the weekly assignments from a friend in the year above. "It's just practice," he says. "The grade barely counts, and I'll learn it properly when I actually need it."

Five years later, Daniel is working at a firm that designs pedestrian bridges.

### Decision point

Was it really "just practice"?

### Debrief

- **A. Yes, it's only an assignment.** The grade really was small. But assignments are where skills are built, and the skill is what the degree promises.
- **B. No, the skills matter later.** Daniel's degree tells his employer, and the people who walk over his bridges, that he can do these calculations. If he skipped learning them, that promise is empty, even if nobody ever finds out.
- **C. It depends on the course.** Some courses matter more for a career than others. But it's hard to know in advance which skills you'll need, and habits of cutting corners tend to spread.
- **D. Only the final exam matters.** Exams test some skills, but they can't check everything. Much of what a degree guarantees is learned in the everyday work in between.

Case 1 showed that cheating harms other people's degrees. Case 2 shows it can harm the people who rely on what your degree says you can do.

### Your turn

Write one or two sentences you could use to explain to a first-year student why academic integrity protects the value of their own degree. Keep it in your own words, as if you were talking to them over coffee.

### Go deeper (for postgraduates and PhDs)

In research, the same values decide whether results can be trusted. A fabricated or falsified study doesn't only damage its authors' careers. Other researchers may build on it, funding may be wasted, and in fields like medicine, decisions affecting patients may rest on it. That's why research integrity is treated as a professional duty, not just a personal choice.

### Takeaway

Integrity is shared. A degree is only worth as much as everyone's trust in it.

## Unit 2.2 Fairness in a Group Project

*About 11 minutes. You'll practise handling unfairness in group work in a way that's honest without being harsh.*

### Why group work tests integrity

Group work is where fairness, trust and responsibility meet. Everyone's grade depends on everyone else, and the rules are often less clear than in individual work. Problems usually don't start with cheating. They start with someone not pulling their weight, and others deciding how to react.

### Case file

You're in a group of four for a project worth 40% of the course grade. The deadline is in a week. Three of you have written your sections. The fourth member, Jonas, has missed every meeting and sent one paragraph copied from the course slides. In the group chat he writes:

> "Sorry guys, crazy month. Just put my name on it, I'll make it up next time 🙏"

### Decision point

What do you do?

### Debrief

- **A. Cover for him.** It's kind, and it avoids conflict a week before the deadline. But putting his name on work he didn't do means presenting the work as something it isn't. It's also unfair to the other two members and to students in other groups.
- **B. Talk to him directly.** Often the best first step. Ask what's going on, because a "crazy month" can mean real problems, and agree on something specific he can still do this week. If he can't contribute, be honest that the group will tell the teacher how the work was split.
- **C. Tell the teacher.** Reasonable if talking to him hasn't worked, or if there's no time left. Teachers usually prefer to hear early rather than after grading. Going straight to the teacher without talking to Jonas first can damage trust in the group.
- **D. Document who did what.** A short contribution log (who wrote which section, who attended which meeting) is fair to everyone. Many teachers ask for one anyway. It works best combined with B, so it isn't a surprise to Jonas.

One more thing to know: if someone in a group deletes shared files, hides sources or blocks others from contributing, that's called obstruction. It's also a form of academic misconduct, not just bad teamwork.

### Case file 2

Same group, same problem. This time you've already talked to Jonas, and nothing changed. You email the lecturer, who replies:

> "I'm preparing you for the real world. In a job, nobody solves your team problems for you. I only grade the final product, so sort it out among yourselves."

Some lecturers are supportive and happy to help with exactly this kind of problem. This one isn't.

### Decision point

What now?

### Debrief

- **A. Do his part and keep his name on it.** It gets the project done, and the lecturer said they don't care about the process. But the submission still says four people did the work when three did. The lecturer's attitude doesn't change what's honest.
- **B. Do his part, and state who did what.** Add a short, factual contribution note to the submission, even if nobody asked for it. It's honest, and it protects you if questions come up later. Keep it neutral: facts, not complaints.
- **C. Go to the programme coordinator or student support.** If the problem is serious, for example if it affects your grade or your wellbeing, you can ask someone above the lecturer how group work issues should be handled. Many institutions have rules on this, even when individual lecturers don't follow them.
- **D. Agree a team deal and give Jonas a final chance.** Set one concrete task with a clear deadline, in writing, and agree as a group what happens if he misses it. That's how teams in many workplaces handle it, which is exactly what the lecturer asked for.

The lecturer's point isn't wrong: dealing with team problems is a real skill. But "sort it out yourselves" never means "pretend everyone contributed." You can manage the problem yourselves and still be honest about it.

### Your turn

Write the message you would send to Jonas, either in the group chat or privately. Two to four sentences is enough. Try to be honest and clear without being harsh.

### Takeaway

Fairness doesn't mean being harsh. Be honest early, and keep a record of who did what.

## Unit 2.3 Courage

*About 7 minutes. You'll explore what courage looks like in everyday academic life, and why it rarely means a big public moment.*

### What courage means here

Courage is the value that holds the others together. It's easy to be honest and fair when nothing is at stake. Courage is doing it when it costs you something: a better grade, a moment of awkwardness, or the approval of your classmates. It usually looks quiet, like a private message or a simple "no, thanks", not a speech.

### Case file

Sofia gets her essay back with one of the best grades in the class. Reading it again a week later, she notices a paragraph she took almost word for word from an online article while drafting. She meant to add the reference, but forgot.

Nobody has noticed, and the grades are already final.

### Decision point

What should Sofia do?

### Debrief

- **A. Say nothing.** Nobody noticed, and it was an honest mistake. But her grade now rests partly on someone else's words, and Sofia knows it. If it comes to light later, an unexplained case looks much worse than one she raised herself.
- **B. Tell the teacher and ask how to fix it.** This takes courage, and it's usually the best option. Teachers tend to treat a mistake the student reports very differently from one they discover themselves. Sofia can explain what happened and ask whether she should submit a corrected version.
- **C. Quietly learn from it for next time.** Better habits are worth building, like noting sources while drafting. But this fixes the future, not this essay.
- **D. Ask a friend what to do.** Talking it through can help her find the courage, and a friend might point her to a study advisor. Just don't let it become a way of putting off the decision.

Courage isn't only about standing up to others. Sometimes it means owning your own mistake when staying quiet would be easier. Correcting mistakes is also part of responsibility, one of the six values.

### Your turn

Sofia knows you're an ambassador and sends you a message:

> "I think I should tell my teacher, but I'm scared it'll make things worse. What would you do?"

Write your reply in two to four sentences. Remember Module 1: listen without judging, don't decide for her, and point her to support if she needs it.

### Takeaway

Courage includes owning your own mistakes, even when nobody would notice.

## Unit 2.4 Integrity in Everyday Life

*About 8 minutes. You'll see that the same values apply beyond assignments, and practise presenting yourself strongly without crossing the line.*

### Beyond the classroom

The values from this module don't stop at the university gate. CVs, motivation letters, references, volunteer certificates and even sports results all rely on the same trust. Habits you build in your studies, whether honest or not, tend to follow you into work and everyday life.

### Case file

You're applying for a competitive internship. A friend looks at your CV and says:

> "You need to sell yourself more. Write that you're fluent in German, not B1. Say you led the student project, not that you were part of it. And add the volunteering you're planning to do next month, it's basically done."

### Decision point

Where is the line?

### Debrief

- **A. Rounding up is normal.** Many people do it, which makes it feel harmless. But employers do check, through interviews, tests and references. A claim like "fluent German" can fall apart in the first conversation, and then everything else on your CV is in doubt too.
- **B. Only exact facts.** Honest and safe, but if you're too modest, you can undersell real strengths. Integrity doesn't mean hiding what you're good at.
- **C. It depends on the field.** Expectations about how you present yourself do vary between fields and countries. But no field accepts claims that simply aren't true.
- **D. Framing is fine, inventing isn't.** This is the line most people find useful. Choosing which true things to highlight and describing them confidently is framing. Claiming a level, role or experience you don't have is inventing.

In this case, the German level and the planned volunteering are inventions. "Led the project" depends on what you actually did: if you coordinated the team, say so specifically.

### Your turn

**Quick quiz: everyday dilemmas.** Six short situations from everyday life. For each one, pick what you would most likely do, or write your own answer. There are no right answers.&#32;

1. After paying at the supermarket, you notice the cashier forgot to charge you for one item.
   - Say nothing, it was their mistake
   - Go back and pay for it
   - Tell a friend, but do nothing
   - Something else
2. You accidentally receive an email with confidential information about a colleague's performance.
   - Ignore it and delete the email
   - Use it to your advantage
   - Let the sender or HR know
   - Something else
3. Your neighbour keeps throwing recyclables into the general waste.
   - Ignore it, it's not your responsibility
   - Gently tell them about the recycling rules
   - Report it to the local authority
   - Something else
4. Someone cuts in front of you in a queue.
   - Say nothing, it's easier
   - Politely tell them it's not OK
   - Confront them loudly
   - Something else
5. On a crowded train, someone is struggling with several bags, and you're in a hurry.
   - Ignore them, you have your own priorities
   - Help briefly, even if it slows you down
   - Help fully, even if you miss your train
   - Something else
6. You try to live by honesty, fairness and respect, but today you realise you let a small ethical choice slide.
   - Accept that mistakes happen, even when you try your best
   - Be hard on yourself, you've failed
   - Ignore it, it's not a big deal
   - Something else

*Adapted from "[Everyday life decisions and academic integrity dilemmas](https://www.academicintegrity.eu/materials/412)" by the ENAI Educational Materials Working Group.*

### Takeaway

Present your real strengths confidently. Never invent the ones you don't have.

## Reflection 2

*About 5 minutes. An ENAI representative reads every reflection and replies with written feedback.*

1. Why is academic integrity important to you personally?
2. Why do you think people sometimes fail to live by these values?

A few honest sentences for each question are enough. There are no wrong answers, and your ideas for change may inspire activities ENAI prepares during the year.
