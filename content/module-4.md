# Module 4: Learning with AI

This is the full text for Module 4, written as ambassadors will see it. It covers the difference between AI that supports learning and AI that replaces it, and how rules differ between courses.

## Unit 4.1 Helper or Ghostwriter?

*About 9 minutes. You'll learn to tell the difference between AI that supports your learning and AI that replaces it.*

### AI and integrity

Generative AI can be a great learning tool: it can explain a concept in a new way, quiz you before an exam, or point out unclear sentences. The problem starts when AI does the work that an assignment is meant to show you can do. ENAI calls this **unauthorised content generation (UCG)**: using AI or other digital tools to produce content without permission or without disclosing it. A useful question is: *after using AI, have I learned what this assignment is for, or has the AI done it for me?*

In 2026, ENAI launched the **Dubai Accord on Academic Integrity in the Age of AI** at its Summer School in Dubai. Developed with students directly involved, it's a global framework of shared values and commitments that aims to make sure AI strengthens learning, research and public trust rather than weakening them. It isn't a misconduct code. It's a common ethical floor covering authorship, disclosure, assessment, privacy, equity and human oversight. It adds two values to the six you met in Module 2, transparency and human agency, and includes a section on student rights and responsibilities.

Read the Accord on the [Dubai Accord webpage](https://www.academicintegrity.eu/wp/dubai-accord-on-academic-integrity-in-the-age-of-artificial-intelligence/). If you agree with it, you can also endorse it there as an individual.

### Case file

Four students in the same course use AI for the same essay:

- **Mia** asks AI to suggest possible angles on the topic, then chooses one and writes everything herself.
- **Karim** writes the essay himself, then uses an AI tool to correct grammar and spelling.
- **Lena** doesn't read the set articles. She asks AI to summarise them and writes the essay from the summaries.
- **Ivan** gives AI the assignment brief and submits what it writes, with small edits.

### Decision point

Where does the line get crossed first?

### Debrief

- **A. Already with Mia.** Some courses do expect students to develop their own ideas from scratch, so brainstorming with AI may not be allowed everywhere. But in most cases, Mia's thinking and writing are her own.
- **B. With Karim.** Grammar correction is widely accepted, and many institutions allow it, sometimes with disclosure. It can matter in language courses, where correct grammar is what's being assessed.
- **C. With Lena.** Often where the real line is. Lena's writing is her own, but she skipped the reading the essay is meant to show. Summaries can also be wrong or miss key points, and she wouldn't know.
- **D. Only with Ivan.** Ivan is clearly over the line: the essay is AI's work, not his. But stopping at Ivan misses the quieter problem with Lena.

AI use sits on a spectrum, and the line depends on what the assignment is meant to show. That's why the course rules matter so much, which is the topic of Unit 4.2.

### Your turn: learning or replacing?

For each use, decide: does it support learning, should you ask first, or does it replace learning?

1. AI explains a concept you didn't understand in the lecture
2. AI writes the discussion section of your lab report
3. AI quizzes you on the course material before an exam
4. AI translates your essay, written in your first language, into English
5. AI generates the reference list for your essay

**How others see it:** 1 and 3 usually support learning. 4 is "ask first", because in some courses the writing in English is what's assessed. 2 replaces learning. 5 is risky: AI often invents references that don't exist, so every source must be checked against the real publication.

### Go deeper (for postgraduates and PhDs)

In research, journals and publishers increasingly have their own AI policies. Many, along with the Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), state that AI tools can't be listed as authors, because they can't take responsibility for the work. Most expect authors to disclose how AI was used, for example in the methods or acknowledgements.

### Takeaway

Ask yourself: did AI help me learn, or did it do the learning for me?

## Unit 4.2 Different Course, Different Rules

*About 7 minutes. You'll learn how to find out which AI rules apply, and what to do when nobody has said anything.*

### Why the rules differ

There's no single rule on AI. Your institution may have a general policy, each course may set its own rules, and an individual assignment may have specific instructions. In most cases, the most specific instruction applies: if the assignment brief says "no AI", that counts even if the general policy is more relaxed. Rules are also changing fast, so last year's answer may no longer be right.

### Case file

Erik is taking three courses this semester:

- In **Marketing**, AI is allowed if students disclose how they used it.
- In **Statistics**, AI is not allowed at all.
- In **Sociology**, the teacher has never mentioned AI, and nothing is written in the course materials.

Erik has a Sociology essay due next week.

### Decision point

What should Erik do in Sociology?

### Debrief

- **A. Assume it's allowed.** Silence feels like permission, and some teachers may indeed not mind. But if the institution's general policy restricts AI, silence doesn't override it, and "nobody told me" is a weak defence.
- **B. Assume it's banned.** The safest guess, and it can't get him into trouble. But he might miss out on uses that would be perfectly fine and helpful.
- **C. Ask the teacher.** The best option. A short email gets a clear answer, often in writing. Teachers who haven't thought about it yet are usually glad to be asked.
- **D. Use it and disclose it.** Being open is good, and it's far better than hidden use. But disclosure doesn't make something allowed. If AI turns out to be banned, disclosing it means admitting a breach.

### Your turn

Write short answers to three questions about whether AI rules should be the same for every subject:

1. Name one subject where AI could genuinely help learning, and say why.
2. Name one subject where AI should be banned, at least for some tasks, and say why.
3. Decide: universal rules, subject-specific rules, or a mix? Explain in one or two sentences.

**A tip for thinking it through:** ask what each subject is meant to teach. If the skill is the point of the course, like translating in a language course or calculating by hand in basic maths, AI doing it replaces the learning. If the skill is something else, like interpreting data in a sociology project, AI may free up time for what really matters. There's no right answer, and after you submit, you'll see how other ambassadors answered.

### Takeaway

When the rules are unclear, ask. And remember that disclosure doesn't replace permission.

## Unit 4.3 How to Disclose

*About 8 minutes. You'll learn what a good AI disclosure includes, so you can explain it to others and write one yourself.*

### What good disclosure looks like

When AI use is allowed, disclosing it is part of being honest about your work. A good disclosure answers four questions: **which tool** you used, **what for**, **which parts** of the work it affected, and **how you checked** the result. Some institutions or teachers give a template or require you to attach your prompts, so check their instructions first.

### Case file

Hana's course allows AI with disclosure. She wrote her essay herself, then asked an AI tool to suggest a better structure and clearer wording for a few paragraphs. She accepted some suggestions and rejected others. Now she isn't sure what to write.

### Decision point

How should Hana disclose her AI use?

### Debrief

- **A. Nothing, it was minor.** It can feel too small to mention. But restructuring an essay isn't minor, and her course requires disclosure. Leaving it out turns allowed use into hidden use.
- **B. Just write "AI was used".** Better than nothing, but it leaves the teacher guessing. Did AI write the essay or fix two commas?
- **C. A short, specific note.** The best option in most cases: which tool, what she used it for, which parts it affected, and that she checked and edited the suggestions herself.
- **D. Attach all her prompts.** Very transparent, and some teachers ask for it. Without such a request, it's usually more than needed, but she should keep the prompts in case questions come up.

### Your turn

Write the disclosure statement for Hana's essay in two or three sentences. Then compare it with this example:

> "I used \[tool name\] to suggest a clearer structure for my essay and better wording in paragraphs 2 and 4. I decided which suggestions to accept and rewrote them in my own words. All ideas, arguments and sources are my own."

### Takeaway

A good disclosure says which tool, what for, which parts, and how you checked.

## Unit 4.4 Falsely Flagged

*About 8 minutes. You'll learn why AI detectors can be wrong, and how students can protect themselves by keeping a record of their writing process.*

### What AI detectors can and can't do

AI detectors estimate how likely it is that a text was written by AI. They can't prove it. Their results can be wrong in both directions, and studies have found that they flag texts by non-native writers more often. A detector score should be a reason to talk with the student, not a verdict. The best protection for students is evidence of how they wrote: drafts, notes and version history.

### Case file

Yusuf is an international student who writes in English as his second language. He worked on his essay for two weeks. A week after submitting, he receives an email from his teacher:

> "An AI detection tool rated your essay as 78% likely to be AI-generated. Please explain."

He didn't use AI, and he's shocked.

### Decision point

What should Yusuf do?

### Debrief

- **A. Accept it, it's his word against the tool.** It can feel hopeless, but a detector score isn't proof. Accepting a finding for something he didn't do could have consequences for his record.
- **B. Show drafts and version history.** Usually the strongest response. Version history in Google Docs or Word shows the essay growing over time, which AI-generated text pasted in at once doesn't.
- **C. Ask for a meeting to explain his process.** A good step alongside B. Talking through how he researched and wrote the essay, and answering questions about it, shows that he knows his work.
- **D. Get advice before replying formally.** If he feels unsure or anxious, a study advisor or the student union can explain the procedure and his rights, and may even join him in a meeting. It helps him respond calmly and with confidence.

As an ambassador, you can help a student in Yusuf's position stay calm, gather evidence, and find out about their rights and the support available.&#32;

### Your turn: protect your process

Open the writing tool you use most, such as Google Docs or Word, and find where its version history is. Then write down three habits you'll use from now on to keep evidence of your writing process, for example:

- Write in one document with version history switched on
- Save notes and sources as you go
- If you use AI where allowed, keep the chats

### Takeaway

A detector score isn't proof. Your drafts and version history are your best evidence.
